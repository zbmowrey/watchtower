# Copywriting frameworks catalog

The persuasion and structure depth behind `SKILL.md`. Load this when you want the fuller catalog
or the sources. Everything here is filtered through the house rule: keep the principle, drop the
hype. Specificity and honest proof do the persuading; superlatives and manufactured urgency do
not.

## 1. Match the message to the reader (Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966)

The single highest-value idea here. Two scales decide how you open and how long you go.

**Five states of awareness** (what the reader already knows; sets the lead and the length):

1. **Unaware:** feels no problem. Lead with a story, an identity, or a surprising truth; name the
   problem before any product.
2. **Problem-aware:** feels the pain, knows no solution category. Dramatize the pain, then
   introduce the category.
3. **Solution-aware:** knows solutions exist, not yours. Lead with why your approach wins.
4. **Product-aware:** knows you, not convinced. Lead with differentiation, the unique mechanism,
   and proof.
5. **Most-aware:** ready to act. Lead with the name, the offer, the price. Say little else.

Rule: the more aware the reader, the shorter the pitch. This is also how to resolve the "long copy
vs short copy" argument: length is set by what the reader needs to decide, never by a word-count
goal, and copy of any length must still be scannable and padding-free.

**Five stages of market sophistication** (how tired the claims are, set by competitors):

1. First to market: state the claim plainly; name the benefit.
2. Competitors copy it: enlarge or sharpen the claim.
3. Claims are tired: shift from *what* to *how* by introducing a **named mechanism** (the method
   behind the result). For a plain voice this is the best move in a crowded market, because a real
   mechanism is specificity, not hype.
4. Mechanism copied: elaborate it (easier, faster, more complete).
5. Market exhausted and cynical: claims fail; resonate on identity, not features.

## 2. Structures (use as scaffolds, not cages)

**AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action).** Origin: E. St. Elmo Lewis (~1898); acronym coined
by C.P. Russell (1921), cemented by E.K. Strong (1925). Map a page top to bottom: headline =
Attention, subhead/lead = Interest, proof and outcome = Desire, button = Action. Descriptive
heuristic, not an experimental result.

**PAS (Problem, Agitate, Solution).** Popularized by Dan Kennedy. Name the reader's problem,
intensify the felt cost of leaving it unsolved, then present the solution as relief. Works for a
real, already-felt pain. Ethics: agitate a pain that is true. Sharpen what is real; never
manufacture fear. The loss-aversion basis is evidence-backed; the formula itself is craft lore.

**FAB (Features, Advantages, Benefits) and the "so what?" ladder.** Feature is the fact; advantage
is what it does; benefit is the outcome the reader gets. Keep asking "so what?" of a feature until
you reach a real outcome. Caveat that matters for a plain voice: do not launder concrete specifics
into vague benefit-speak. "Backs up every 5 minutes" beats "peace of mind." The best line keeps
the number and names the payoff.

**The Rule of One.** One reader, one big idea, one promise, one CTA per page. Distilled from the
direct-response tradition (Caples, Ogilvy). Before writing, name the single reader and single
promise; cut anything serving a second idea; one primary CTA (repeated is fine, competing is not).

## 3. Customer-centered framing

**StoryBrand SB7 (Donald Miller, *Building a StoryBrand*, 2017).** Cast the **customer as the
hero and the brand as the guide.** Seven beats: a Character who has a Problem meets a Guide
(empathy plus authority) who gives a Plan and a call to Action, ending in Success and helping
avoid Failure. Mantras: "if you confuse, you lose," and the **grunt test** (a stranger should say
back, within about five seconds, what you offer, how it helps, and how to get it). Popular
commercial framework, not validated research; the clarity and customer-as-hero principles are
sound. Treat the seven steps as a checklist, not law.

**Jobs-to-be-Done (Clayton Christensen).** Customers "hire" a product to make **progress** in a
situation. Speak to the job and the struggle, not the demographic. The milkshake case: commuters
hired a thick shake to make a boring one-handed commute longer and more interesting, a job no
demographic survey revealed. In copy: lead with the situation and the progress ("for the long
commute..."), not a feature list.

## 4. Value propositions

**Geoffrey Moore positioning template** (*Crossing the Chasm*): "For [target] who [need], the
[product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], our product [differentiation]."
Forces real decisions about customer, category, competitor, and outcome. If you cannot fill
"unlike X," you have hope, not differentiation. Use it as the source-of-truth one-liner the page
expands.

**Value Proposition Canvas** (Osterwalder, *Value Proposition Design*, 2014). Customer profile =
Jobs, Pains, Gains; value map = products/services, pain relievers, gain creators. Mine the
pains/gains/jobs for the literal words the copy should answer; make sure every claim relieves a
named pain or creates a named gain.

**Voice of customer (Joanna Wiebe / Copyhackers).** Do not write copy only from your own head.
Mine real phrasings from reviews, support tickets, and interviews, then write in the customer's
own words about their problems and objections. People convert when they hear their own voice. For
any page, harvest 5 to 10 real user quotes first; favor the customer's noun over your branded one.
Clarity trumps persuasion: if a reader has to decode the line, you have lost them.

## 5. Headlines

The headline does the heaviest lifting. Ogilvy: on average five times as many people read the
headline as read the body, so a weak headline wastes the rest. Caples: headlines win on
self-interest, news, or curiosity, in that order of reliability; "long headlines that say
something outsell short headlines that say nothing." Proven patterns: "How to [outcome]," "Who
else wants [outcome]," "Do you make these mistakes in [domain]."

**The 4 U's** (Michael Masterson): useful, urgent, unique, ultra-specific. Score a headline 1 to 4
on each. Ultra-specific is the lever for a plain voice: swap an adjective for a number or a
concrete detail. Overarching rule: clarity over cleverness; never trade comprehension for a pun.

## 6. CTAs, proof, and objections

**Calls to action.** Specific, action plus value, low friction. Command label that says what
happens next: "Download the report," "Start free trial," "Continue to payment," not "Submit." One
A/B result (Michael Aagaard / Unbounce) found first-person "Start my free trial" beat "Start
your..." by about 90% clicks; later replications show smaller but consistent first-person lifts.
Treat the direction as real, the exact number as one test. Put a risk-reducer near the button
("No card required").

**Social proof and specificity (Cialdini, *Influence*, 1984).** Concrete numbers outperform
adjectives. Duolingo shows "45.6M learners," not "millions." Replace "trusted by many" with named,
numbered, relevant proof the reader can see themselves in. Schottmuller's CRAVENS grades proof
quality: Credible, Relevant, Attractive, Visual, Enumerated, Nearby, Specific.

**Objection handling.** List every hesitation (price, fit, trust, "won't work for me") and preempt
each in the copy before it forms: risk reversal near the CTA, an FAQ, and proof matched to the
specific objection.

## 7. The advertising canon (the durable principles)

- **Claude Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising* (1923).** Copy is salesmanship in print: every word
  earns its keep by moving the reader. "Definite figures are believed; superlatives are
  discounted." Give the reason a claim is true, then test it. This is the original settlement of
  hype vs plainness, and it lands on the side of specifics.
- **David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* / *Confessions*.** The headline carries the ad; facts
  beat adjectives (the Rolls-Royce "electric clock" line sells with one fact, zero adjectives);
  interested prospects will read long copy, so do not truncate substance. Start from research.
- **Eugene Schwartz** (section 1).
- **John Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods* (1932)** (section 5).
- **Joe Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*.** The slippery slide: the sole purpose of the
  first sentence is to get the second one read, and so on. Open short and frictionless. "You sell
  on emotion, but you justify the purchase with logic," so pair the felt benefit with a proof
  point.
- **Robert Cialdini, *Influence*.** Seven principles, each honest only when the underlying fact is
  true: reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity. Use
  them as honesty checks, not as tricks.
- **Robert Bly (*The Copywriter's Handbook*) and Gary Halbert (*The Boron Letters*).** Write to
  one reader in the second person, lead with their benefit, and know the market cold before you
  write a word.

## 8. Writing for the screen (web and UX)

- **People scan in an F-shape** (NN/g eyetracking): two horizontal sweeps then a vertical scan
  down the left. Front-load information-carrying words at the start of headings, paragraphs, and
  sentences; never bury the point mid-sentence.
- **Inverted pyramid** (NN/g): conclusion first; interest wanes in the middle and many never reach
  the end. The first sentence of each paragraph and the first words of each sentence carry the
  most weight.
- **Be succinct** (NN/g): onscreen reading is slower and users skim, so cut text roughly in half
  versus print; use two or three heading levels, highlighted keywords, and short paragraphs.
- **Microcontent stands alone** (NN/g): headings, links, and subject lines are read out of context
  in scans, search results, and menus, so each must be self-explanatory. No "click here" or bare
  "learn more"; link text says where it goes.
- **Make it self-evident** (Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think*): treat a page like a billboard read
  at 60 mph. "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what's left." If a
  sentence needs a second read, rewrite it.
- **Plain language** (plainlanguage.gov / Plain Writing Act): short sentences (average under 20
  words), common everyday words, active voice over half the time, "you" for the reader and "we"
  for the org, logical order, no unexplained jargon or acronyms. Target about grade 6 to 8 for
  broad audiences; run a readability check.
- **Microcopy.** CTA labels are verb plus object matched to the user's goal and resulting state.
  Error messages use human language (no "invalid/illegal"), do not blame the user, say what
  happened, offer a concrete fix, and preserve input ("We couldn't find an account for that email.
  Check the spelling or create a new account."). Empty states explain what goes here and give one
  clear next action.

## 9. Brand voice and tone

- **Voice vs tone.** Voice is the brand's constant personality across every touchpoint; tone is
  how it flexes by context and the reader's emotional state. "You have the same voice all the
  time, but your tone changes" (Mailchimp). Errors are not written like onboarding.
- **NN/g four dimensions of tone of voice:** formal vs casual, serious vs funny, respectful vs
  irreverent, enthusiastic vs matter-of-fact. Score each piece on all four to make tone an
  explicit, repeatable decision.
- **NN/g tone research:** about 52% of the variability in how desirable copy feels is explained by
  trustworthiness; friendliness adds roughly 8%, and a too-playful tone can erode credibility. A
  conversational tone tested as both friendlier and more trustworthy even in austere sectors.
  Default to clear and trustworthy; spend personality carefully.
- **The voice chart ("we are X, not Y").** Define voice as 3 to 5 attributes, each with a
  contrasting anti-attribute and concrete do/don't examples. Naming the lines you will not cross
  is as load-bearing as the positives. Every attribute needs a sample rewrite.
- **The plain-language style-guide cluster.** Mailchimp (plainspoken, genuine, demystify jargon,
  dry humor; "more important to be clear than entertaining"), Atlassian (bold, optimistic,
  practical, with "appropriate delight" only after trust is earned; dial down boldness when users
  are confused), Shopify Polaris ("just focus on sounding human"; verb-first instructions; read it
  aloud), Apple HIG and Google Material (clear, direct, jargon-free, active voice, second person,
  blame-free errors). These converge on plain plus active plus second-person plus blame-free.
- **Brand archetypes** (Jung via Mark and Pearson, *The Hero and the Outlaw*, 2001): twelve
  archetypes (Innocent, Sage, Explorer, Hero, Outlaw, Magician, Everyman, Lover, Jester,
  Caregiver, Creator, Ruler) grouped by four motivations (independence, mastery, belonging,
  stability). A fast way to seed a personality. Be honest: it is a resonant heuristic rooted in
  Jungian theory, not validated science. Use it to seed attributes, not to prove anything.
- **Tone flexes to the reader's emotional state.** Calm and blame-free in errors, brief and warm
  at success, patient and prescriptive for new users, plain and exact for billing or legal. Voice
  stays constant.

## 10. Evidence tiers and sources

**Backed by data or tests:** specificity beats superlatives (Hopkins), the headline's outsized
readership (Ogilvy), F-pattern and inverted-pyramid scanning (NN/g), trust-dominates-tone (NN/g),
first-person CTA lift and social-proof conversion lifts (case studies, directional).

**Sound theory, qualitatively grounded:** Cialdini's persuasion principles, Jobs-to-be-Done, the
Moore and Osterwalder tools, the voice/tone distinction, Schwartz's awareness and sophistication.

**Durable craft heuristics, popular but not experimentally proven:** AIDA, PAS, FAB, the Rule of
One, StoryBrand SB7, the 4 U's, brand archetypes. Use them to structure; let specificity, real
pains, and concrete proof do the persuading.
